Breathing Exercises for Sleep: The Longer Exhale at Bedtime

Breathing exercises for sleep that fit around your toothbrush: a 4-minute wind-down with a longer exhale, then a low hum.

By Maya Chen · 7 min read

Breathe out longer than you breathe in.

It's 10:40 at night. You've been tired since the middle of the afternoon, and now that you're standing in the bathroom with the light on, your mind is wide awake. It's replaying the call you handled badly and sorting tomorrow's list. You're exhausted, but you can't turn off.

If you've gone looking for breathing exercises for sleep on a night like this, here is one that fits around brushing your teeth: a 4-minute wind-down, with two minutes of breathing on a longer exhale and then two minutes of humming.

Many people describe this same gap between tired and rested. One way to picture it: your body hasn't received a signal that the day is done. The dishes, the phone and the overhead lights all say keep going. An evening practice is a way to send that signal on purpose.

Why breathing exercises for sleep lean on the exhale

Your heart speeds up slightly as you breathe in and slows slightly as you breathe out. The name for that rhythm is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and your vagus nerve is part of it (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). A longer exhale means you spend more of each breath in the slowing half. That is the plain logic behind "in for 4, out for 8."

The research on the ratio itself is mixed. In a small 2014 experiment with 30 people, Van Diest and colleagues found that people reported more relaxation when the exhale was the longer part of the breath than when the inhale was. In 2024, Meehan and Shaffer compared a 1:2 ratio with even breaths at six breaths a minute and found no difference in heart rate variability.

Neither study measured sleep. Take this breath as a way to slow down at the end of the day, with no promise about how fast you'll drift off. I go through both studies in two breathing exercises to calm down when you're stressed, the daytime version of this breath.

You don't need to hit a perfect number. The key is that the exhale is longer than the inhale.

Why the second half is a hum

A hum is a long exhale with sound in it. You can't hum without letting your breath out slowly, so it keeps the longer exhale going while your hands are busy with pajamas and covers.

In one 2013 study, healthy young adults hummed a tone, sang a hymn and sang a slow mantra, and singing with a regular structure brought their heart rate into step with their breathing (Vickhoff et al., 2013). That study was about singing and it didn't look at sleep, so hold it loosely.

If counting your breath tends to wind you up, humming gives you a slow exhale with no numbers in it. On those nights you can just hum: mouth closed, a low note, 2 minutes.

Before you begin

A safety note: if you have asthma or COPD, check with your physician before practicing extended exhale breathing. If you're prone to lightheadedness or hyperventilation, go slowly and shorten the exhale at first. If you feel dizzy, return to normal breathing right away. And if you carry trauma or tend to dissociate, breathwork can occasionally feel activating rather than settling. You can stop and breathe naturally at any point.

If counting or watching your breath raises your anxiety, skip the breathing step and use the humming on its own for the first week. Humming alone is a complete evening practice. If you have a vocal cord injury or recent throat surgery, check with your physician before humming.

The 4-minute wind-down, step by step

Sit on the edge of your bed or on a bathroom stool. The practice itself takes 4 minutes: 2 minutes of breathing and 2 minutes of humming. Brushing your teeth in the middle and the three closing breaths in bed aren't counted, so allow a few minutes more on the clock.

  1. Settle. Feel your feet on the floor. Take one breath without managing it, and notice its quality: is it shallow, held or rapid?
  2. Breathe in for 4, out for 8. Inhale through your nose for 4 slow counts. Exhale through your nose or slightly parted lips for 8 slow counts. If 8 feels strained, use 6. Keep your jaw soft and let your hands rest open on your thighs. Continue for 2 minutes, roughly 8 to 10 cycles.
  3. Brush your teeth. Let your breath find its own pace while you brush, slow if that comes easily. No humming yet.
  4. Hum. Put the toothbrush away, close your mouth and hum on a comfortable low note: a song, a single tone or a simple "hmmmm." Breathe in through your nose whenever you need to. Feel the vibration in your chest and throat. Continue for 2 minutes while you change, fold back the covers and dim the lights.
  5. Close. Lie down and take three slow breaths with a long exhale. Set your phone face down or outside the room. Notice the weight of your body against the mattress. Rest.

The evening version in my book uses 4 in and 8 out, with 6 as the fallback, so the exhale can sit anywhere from 6 to 8 counts. If this breath is new to you, start at 4 in and 6 out, which is where the daytime version starts, and move to 8 once that feels easy. Leave the inhale at 4. If any count feels forced, drop to 4 in and 5 out and build from there.

This wind-down is one half of a daily routine in my book, Vagus Nerve Exercises for Anxiety. The book adds the six-minute morning sequence and a 28-day log where you rate your anxiety from 0 to 5 each day, so you can see on paper whether anything is shifting.

What you might notice

Your thoughts may slow down during the humming, or your arms and legs may feel heavy. Restlessness or an urge to check your phone can show up too, and it may ease if you stay with the hum a little longer.

Sometimes sadness or warmth surfaces when the day finally goes quiet. You can let it be there, and if it feels like too much, open your eyes, stop and breathe normally.

You might also notice nothing. The book asks you to come back after 3 evenings and see what is different about falling asleep, even slightly, and it counts "nothing" and "I keep forgetting" as useful answers.

If lying flat is uncomfortable, the closing breaths work propped on pillows or in a recliner. If you feel self-conscious humming near a partner, keep it barely audible. A quiet hum still stretches out your exhale. And if the breathing leaves you dizzy or short of air, go back to normal breathing, then try a shorter exhale or the humming alone tomorrow.

When to use it

  • At your toothbrush, every night. The breathing goes just before you brush and the humming just after.
  • In bed, on the nights you forgot. You can do it lying down, half asleep. On the hardest days, 2 minutes of humming before sleep is enough to keep the habit alive.
  • When you wake in the small hours. The same breath works lying down in the dark: in for 4, out for 8, before you reach for your phone or check the time.

For the nights you're already lying down with the lamp off, there are three vagus nerve exercises in bed: a fist squeeze, a jaw-to-heels release and a quiet hum.

Questions women ask about breathing exercises for sleep

How long should breathing exercises for sleep take? This one takes 4 minutes: 2 minutes of breathing in for 4 and out for 8, then 2 minutes of humming. On the hardest days, 2 minutes of humming before sleep is enough to keep the habit alive.

Can I do breathing exercises for sleep lying down? Yes. The breath works lying down in the dark, and on the nights you forgot you can do the wind-down in bed, half asleep. If lying flat is uncomfortable, try the closing breaths propped on pillows or in a recliner.

Will a longer exhale help me fall asleep faster? I can't promise that. The research on the ratio is mixed, and neither study measured sleep.
