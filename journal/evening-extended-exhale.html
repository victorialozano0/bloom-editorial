The Longer Exhale: A Bedtime Practice for Anxiety

A gentle four-minute wind-down that uses a longer exhale and a little humming to tell your nervous system the day is done.

By Maya Chen · 3 min read

Breathe out longer than you breathe in.

You're exhausted, but you can't turn off. Your body wants to collapse and your mind keeps reviewing the day: the call you handled badly, the pile of work waiting for tomorrow.

That gap between tired and rested isn't a character flaw. It's a timing problem. Your nervous system doesn't know the day is over. It is still waiting for an "all clear" signal it never received. An evening practice is a way to send that signal on purpose.

Why the exhale matters

Your heart rate naturally slows a little each time you breathe out. When you make the exhale longer than the inhale, you lean into that slowing. Lengthening the exhale relative to the inhale activates what's called the vagal brake on the heart (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014), and that slowdown registers in your brain as a signal of safety.

You don't need to hit a perfect number. The key is that the exhale is longer than the inhale.

The evening wind-down, step by step

A safety note first: if counting your breath ever triggers anxiety or makes you feel disconnected, use the humming step on its own for the first week. Humming alone is a complete evening practice.

Sit on the edge of your bed or on a bathroom stool. The whole sequence takes about four minutes.

  1. Settle. Feel your feet on the floor. Take one breath without managing it, and notice its quality: is it shallow, held or rapid?
  2. Extend the exhale. Inhale through your nose for 4 slow counts. Exhale through your nose or slightly parted lips for 8 slow counts. If 8 feels strained, try 6. Continue for about 2 minutes (roughly 8 to 10 cycles). Keep your jaw soft and let your hands rest open on your thighs.
  3. Transition. Get up and brush your teeth, continuing to breathe slowly if you can.
  4. Hum. With your mouth closed, hum on a comfortable low note: a song, a single tone or a simple "hmmmm." Feel the vibration in your chest and throat. Continue for 2 minutes while you brush, walk to bed and settle the covers.
  5. Close. Lying down, take three slow extended exhales. Set your phone face down or outside the room. Notice the weight of your body against the mattress. Rest.

What you might notice

Your thoughts may slow during the humming, or your limbs may feel heavy. Both are signs of your body preparing for rest. Restlessness or an urge to check your phone usually passes within about a minute if you stay with the humming. Sometimes unexpected sadness or warmth surfaces as you settle, and that is completely normal.

If lying flat is uncomfortable, the breathing works well propped on pillows or in a recliner. If you feel self-conscious humming near a partner, keep it barely audible. The vibration still travels through the vagus nerve at low volume.

If you take medication for anxiety or sleep, please talk with your doctor before changing anything.

Try it tonight. Before you pick up your toothbrush, give yourself three breaths of 4 counts in and 8 counts out. Then notice what your body does.
