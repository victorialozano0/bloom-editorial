When You Reach for the Phone to Call Her

Grief does not wait for anniversaries. Sometimes it arrives in a parking lot, with your hand already reaching for her number.

By Clara Bennett · 2 min read

Love reaches for her out of habit.

It happens on a Tuesday, or a Sunday afternoon, or in a grocery store parking lot. Something small goes right, or something small goes wrong, and your hand is already moving toward the phone.

You know exactly what you were going to say. You can almost hear how she would answer. The way she said your name when she picked up. The little pause before she asked how you were really doing.

And then you remember.

The phone is still in your hand. Her number is probably still in it, too. You may not have been able to delete it. Nobody needs to know that.

The ordinary moments are the hardest

People expect grief to show up at the funeral, on her birthday, on Mother's Day. It does. But it also arrives in places no one warned you about.

The recipe you cannot quite remember. The song in a waiting room. The sweater on a stranger that looks like one she wore. The good news you want to tell someone, and the only someone you want is her.

These moments are quiet. Nobody around you notices them. You stand in the cereal aisle with your heart dropping through the floor, and the world keeps going.

Reaching for the phone is not a mistake. It is what love does out of habit.

Reaching for the phone is not a mistake. It is what love does out of habit. For years, maybe decades, she was the person you called. Your hand remembers that, even when the rest of you knows better.

You do not have to stop reaching. You do not have to train yourself out of it. There is nothing here to fix.

Letting the moment be what it is

Some days, the remembering knocks the breath out of you. Some days, it is softer, almost tender, like she was close for a second. Both are real. Neither one means you are doing this wrong.

If you want, you can let the moment have a place to go. A page. A notebook in your bag. A note on your phone that starts with her name. You can write down the thing you were going to tell her, exactly the way you would have said it.

She does not get to answer. That is the part that hurts. But the words still belong to her, and you can still give them somewhere to land.

You are not behind. You are not stuck. You are someone who loved her mother and still does, in the middle of an ordinary day.

A gentle prompt: Think of the last time you reached for the phone to call her. What were you going to tell her? Write it to her now, the way you would have said it out loud.
