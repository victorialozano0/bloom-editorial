Healing Scriptures for the Woman Tired of Asking: Bitter Water

Healing scriptures for the long wait: the God who heals at bitter water, the woman who reached anyway, and a prayer for flat days.

By Hannah Grace · 7 min read

God named Himself Healer at bitter water.

Three days after the Red Sea, the singing stopped.

If you have gone looking for healing scriptures after a long stretch of asking, begin here, with people who had run out of water and patience at the same time. Israel had just watched God part the sea. Miriam had led the women out with timbrels and dancing. Then they walked three days into the wilderness, found water at last, and could not drink it. It was bitter, and the place was named for it: Marah, which means bitter.

The people murmured against Moses. Moses cried to the LORD, the LORD showed him a tree, and when he cast it into the water, the water was made sweet. There, beside that water, God gives Himself a name:

"For I am the LORD that healeth thee." (Exodus 15:26, KJV)

The God Who Heals at Bitter Water

He speaks this name at Marah itself, to people who were murmuring a few verses earlier and who still have a wilderness to cross.

Healing is not a reward for having finished suffering. It is a name God wears into the middle of it.

If you have prayed for healing for a long time, you may know a quieter ache: the embarrassment of asking again. Picture a woman I'll call Denise. She is a composite, her story gathered from many women's. She has prayed about the same body for three years, and lately she feels she is bothering God, as if repeating one request this many times must mean she is doing something wrong.

It is easy to read healing promises like a vending machine: insert enough faith, receive the outcome. When the outcome doesn't come, we blame our faith. That reading makes God smaller and the suffering larger. A body that has not healed is no verdict on your faith.

Asking God does not compete with seeing your doctor, either. Prayer goes alongside medical care and does not replace it. Keep your appointments, keep taking what you have been prescribed, and pray as you go.

The woman who reached anyway

Mark tells of a woman who had been bleeding for twelve years, and she had tried everything. She "had suffered many things of many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was nothing bettered, but rather grew worse." (Mark 5:26, KJV)

Mark names the physicians to show how long and how costly her asking had been, with no reproach for having gone to them.

She didn't push to the front. She came up in the crowd behind Jesus and "touched his garment" (Mark 5:27, KJV). Matthew remembers that it was "the hem of his garment" (Matthew 9:20, KJV).

Jesus stopped and looked for her. She came forward "fearing and trembling," and she "told him all the truth" (Mark 5:33, KJV). He answered: "Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague." (Mark 5:34, KJV)

Daughter. She expected to be exposed, and she was given a family name.

Her faith that day looked like a hand going out through a crowd after twelve years of nothing working. Mark gives us her story as hers, and it sets no schedule for yours. Some women reach for years and are still sick, and their reaching is no smaller than hers. Some days, the prayer is just the reaching.

Five more healing scriptures to write out or pray

Exodus 15:26 is the first. Chapter 3 of the book builds a seven-day prayer on it and on passages like these. Think of them as healing scriptures for women who are tired of asking: words to copy onto a card or pray slowly. None of them is a formula, and none comes with a date.

  • Psalm 103:2-3. "Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits: Who forgiveth all thine iniquities; who healeth all thy diseases" (KJV) The hard word is "all." Many illnesses are not healed in this life, and you may know that in your own body. You can pray this verse and say so in the same breath.
  • Psalm 147:3. "He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds." (KJV) For the hurt no scan will show.
  • Jeremiah 17:14. "Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved: for thou art my praise." (KJV) A prayer to borrow whole when you have no words of your own.
  • 2 Corinthians 12:9. "And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness." (KJV) Paul had asked three times for his thorn to be taken away, and it stayed. For whatever has stayed with you.
  • 1 Peter 2:24. "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed." (KJV) Peter is writing about the cross and about sin. Pray it as thanks for what is already finished.

If you want to keep copying verses by hand, the 2027 scripture writing plan sets aside October for eight passages on healing and comfort.

A practice for the flat days

In the book, this is the step for the week when prayer feels like it is landing on a ceiling. Instead of stopping, you tell God about the silence. Set aside 15 to 20 minutes, the time the book gives each day of its seven-day prayer, and bring your Bible, a journal and a pen.

  1. Name what you are bringing. Write one specific sentence. Skip the category ("my health") and write the particular thing, such as the pain that wakes you at 3 a.m. or the diagnosis you are tired of explaining.
  2. Name the absence out loud. Say: "Lord, I don't feel You in this right now. I'm naming that honestly. I'm choosing to stay at Marah anyway." If you are tired of asking, or embarrassed to ask again, tell Him that too.
  3. Read Exodus 15:26 slowly, once. Read it aloud if you can.
  4. Write what comes. Answer one question in a sentence or two: "Lord, where have You been healing me that I haven't acknowledged?" If nothing comes, write that. As the book puts it, the blank page in front of God is still a prayer.
  5. Copy one verse by hand. Choose the verse that landed hardest and write it out word for word. Under it, add one sentence about why it hit you.

If the hurt underneath has never been said out loud at all, begin with The Wound You Stopped Naming, a short way into lament from Lamentations 3.

What you might notice, and when to use it

The book asks you to watch for what shifts in your posture toward the trouble while the trouble itself stays the same. You may feel a little less embarrassed about asking. You may cry. You may notice nothing at all, and nothing is an honest result that needs no fixing.

This practice does not make a body well, and you cannot fail it. If it stirs up more than you can carry alone, stop and tell someone you trust. James tells the church to "pray one for another" (James 5:16, KJV).

  • The night before an appointment or a test result, alongside whatever your doctor has asked you to do.
  • The morning you catch yourself skipping prayer because you have asked so many times already.
  • Once a day for a week, with a different scripture from this page each day.

Questions women ask about healing scriptures

Where does the Bible call God a healer? In Exodus 15:26, beside the bitter water of Marah, God names Himself the LORD who heals. He speaks that name to people who were murmuring a few verses earlier and who still had a wilderness to cross.

Is it wrong to keep asking God for healing? No. The woman in Mark 5 had been ill for twelve years, and her faith that day looked like a hand going out through a crowd. If you are tired of asking, or embarrassed to ask again, you can tell Him that too.

Does it mean my faith is weak if I have not been healed? No. A body that has not healed is no verdict on your faith. Paul asked three times for his thorn to be taken away, and it stayed.

The singing had stopped at Marah, and God spoke there anyway.
