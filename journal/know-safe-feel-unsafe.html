Why Can't I Relax? When Your Body Doesn't Feel Safe

Why can't I relax when nothing is wrong? What your body is doing when it doesn't feel safe, and a 1-to-3-minute humming practice to try tonight.

By Maya Chen · 7 min read

Your mind knows. Your body is still catching up.

It's late on an ordinary weeknight. The dishes are done, the house is quiet, and nothing is wrong. You know nothing is wrong. And still your jaw is set, your chest feels tight, and your mind keeps replaying a conversation from last week. Somewhere around the third replay, the question arrives: why can't I relax?

You have already told yourself the obvious thing: you are not in danger. Your body heard it and stayed braced anyway.

If you have ever asked yourself, Why can't I just relax?, I want you to hear this clearly: that question contains a false premise. You're not failing to relax. Your nervous system is doing exactly what it was built to do.

Why can't I relax when I know I'm safe?

Most advice for an anxious mind is aimed at the thinking part of you, the part that can reason, plan and recognize that a threat isn't real. That work matters. The alarm, though, runs in older, automatic parts of your nervous system, and they don't speak in thoughts. They speak in sensation. That may be why you can win the argument with yourself and still feel your chest bracing for impact.

Picture Priya, 38, a composite, not a real person. "I understand my anxiety," she says. "I know it's not rational. And I still wake up every morning with my heart pounding like I've already done something wrong." Her understanding is real. It hasn't reached the part of her that sounds the alarm.

Your body does most of the talking

The vagus nerve is a long nerve that links your brain with your heart, lungs and gut. Most of its traffic runs upward: about 80 to 90 percent of the fibers in the vagus nerve carry signals from the body up to the brain (Breit et al., 2018).

The vagus nerve is the principal relay for sensory information from your lungs, windpipe and voice box, and its sensory fibers are the main sensory supply to your heart (Prescott & Liberles, 2022). So how you are breathing and how your heart is beating are part of what your brain is working with.

When your breath is quick and shallow and your shoulders are up, your brain may be listening to a body that is still reporting trouble. Giving your body something different to report is another way in.

Where this fits in nervous system regulation

Nervous system regulation is an everyday phrase for how your body moves into a stress response and finds its way back out. The best-known map of it comes from polyvagal theory, which Stephen Porges first set out in a 1994 presidential address, published in 1995. It describes three circuits: ventral vagal, sympathetic and dorsal vagal (Porges, 2022). It is a theory, and scientists still debate its premises (Grossman, 2023).

You may recognize yourself swinging between the last two, wired at midnight and flat and heavy by noon, with only short visits to the first. Nervous system regulation for beginners describes the three states in everyday terms and gives you a 3-minute check-in for finding where you are.

Nobody told your body the emergency was over

Think of a smoke detector installed too close to the stove. It goes off when you make toast. The detector works. It was set for a level of danger that made sense somewhere else, and nobody has reset it.

You never decided to stay on alert. At some point, maybe during a long stretch of stress, your body may have learned that staying ready felt safer than being caught off guard. That learning kept you going. As I put it in the book: "The problem isn't that your nervous system made the wrong call. The problem is that nobody ever told it the emergency was over."

So the job tonight is smaller than "relax." It is to send one signal from the body upward, in a language your body uses. A hum can be a way of telling your body what your mind already knows.

A practice that skips the argument: humming

Humming needs no counting and no convincing. It stretches out your exhale without your having to manage it. And it gives you a vibration you can feel in your throat and chest, so your attention has somewhere to rest other than last week's conversation.

The evidence is preliminary. In a 2013 study, healthy young people hummed a single tone, sang a hymn and sang a slow mantra, and singing in regular phrases brought their heart rate into step with their breathing (Vickhoff et al., 2013). That finding is about heart rhythm and singing. I don't take it as evidence that a minute of humming will quiet anxiety.

A safety note first: most people need no special precautions for this one. If you have a vocal cord injury or have had recent throat surgery, check with your doctor first. If turning your attention inward tends to leave you floaty or disconnected, keep your eyes open and go slowly. If you feel lightheaded, stop humming and breathe normally.

Find a comfortable seat. The whole practice takes 1 to 3 minutes.

  1. Settle. Sit comfortably and close your mouth. Take a normal breath in through your nose.
  2. Start the hum. As you exhale, begin humming any single tone, the one that feels most natural. The pitch doesn't matter. Keep the vibration continuous and steady.
  3. Feel it. Notice the vibration in your lips, your throat and your chest. Place one hand on your breastbone to feel it more clearly.
  4. Follow the breath. Hum for the entire length of your exhale. Breathe in through your nose. Hum out again.
  5. Repeat. Continue for 6 to 10 breath cycles, about 1 to 2 minutes.
  6. Listen. After your last hum, sit in silence for 10 to 15 seconds. Notice what has changed.

If humming out loud isn't possible where you are, make a long, quiet "mmm" on each exhale with your lips barely parted.

Humming is Exercise 4 of the 27 in my book, Vagus Nerve Exercises for Anxiety. The book also stretches it into a five-minute daily version, and it has a sequence for the 3 a.m. wake-up, for the nights when the replay starts after you've already been asleep.

What you might notice

You might feel the vibration spread from your throat into your chest and the bones of your face. There may be a little warmth or tingling in your face, or an urge to yawn or sigh at the end. You might feel self-conscious. That's normal, and the practice doesn't need to feel dignified.

You might also notice nothing. That is a common first result and it doesn't mean you did it wrong. If the hum makes you feel more on edge, stop, look around the room and name five things you can see.

When to use it

  • Late at night, when the replay starts and reasoning with it has stopped working.
  • In the car, before you walk into the house or the office.
  • In the shower, on a morning when you wake up already braced.

This week, when you close your laptop and notice your jaw is still set, try three hummed exhales before you stand up. Then notice what your nervous system does.

Questions women ask about "why can't I relax"

Why can't I relax even when nothing is wrong? The alarm runs in older, automatic parts of your nervous system, and they speak in sensation, not in thoughts. That may be why you can know you are safe and still feel your chest bracing. You're not failing to relax.

How long does the humming practice take? The whole practice takes 1 to 3 minutes: 6 to 10 hummed breaths, about 1 to 2 minutes, then 10 to 15 seconds of silence. You might notice nothing the first time. That is a common first result and it doesn't mean you did it wrong.
