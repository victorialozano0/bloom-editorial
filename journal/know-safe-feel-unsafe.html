Why Your Body Doesn't Believe You're Safe

Your mind knows nothing is wrong, yet your chest stays tight. Here is what the vagus nerve has to do with it.

By Maya Chen · 3 min read

Your mind knows. Your body is still catching up.

It's late on an ordinary weeknight. Nothing is wrong, and you know it. And yet your jaw is clenched, your chest feels tight, and your mind keeps replaying a conversation from last week.

If you have ever asked yourself, Why can't I just relax?, I want you to hear this: you are not failing to relax. Your nervous system is doing exactly what it was built to do.

The part of you that lives below thought

Most anxiety advice is aimed at the thinking brain, the part of you that can reason and recognize that a threat isn't real. That work matters. But the system running the alarm sits lower, in the brainstem, and it doesn't speak in thoughts. It speaks in sensation. That is why you can know you're safe and still feel like your body is bracing for impact.

The main line between that system and your organs is the vagus nerve. Its name comes from the Latin for "wandering," and it earns it: it runs from the base of your skull through your throat, heart and lungs, all the way into your gut. When it is working well, your body gets a steady, quiet signal: safe, calm, connected.

The traffic runs mostly upward

Approximately 80% of the fibers in the vagus nerve carry signals from the body up to the brain, not the other way around (Berntson & Cacioppo, 2004). Your gut, heart and lungs are constantly reporting in, and your brain is largely responding to what they tell it.

So when your breath is quick and shallow, your brain reads that as evidence of danger. Telling yourself "I'm fine" is working against the current. Changing what your body is doing changes the data your brain receives.

In 1994, Stephen Porges described three states your nervous system moves through: ventral vagal (safe and social), sympathetic (fight or flight) and dorsal vagal (shutdown). Many anxious people cycle between the last two, wired at midnight and collapsed by noon, with only brief visits to the first.

Your body learned this for a reason

Think of a smoke detector installed too close to the stove. It goes off when you make toast. It isn't broken. It is calibrated for a level of danger that made sense somewhere else.

Bessel van der Kolk (2014) writes that the nervous system encodes its survival strategies below the level of conscious memory. You never decided to stay on alert. Your body decided for you, and that decision kept you going. The problem isn't that it made the wrong call. Nobody ever told it the emergency was over.

A small practice for tonight. Sit with your feet on the floor. Notice your jaw, then your shoulders, then your belly. Ask yourself which state you're closest to right now, and simply name it, with no judgment. Then let one slow exhale out through slightly parted lips, longer than your inhale, and feel your shoulders drop even a little.

These practices are meant to support professional care, not replace it. If you take medication for anxiety, please talk with your doctor before changing anything.
