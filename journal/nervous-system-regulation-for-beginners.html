Nervous System Regulation for Beginners: A 3-Minute Start

New to nervous system regulation? Learn what it means, the three states polyvagal theory describes, and a gentle 3-minute check-in to try today.

By Maya Chen · 5 min read

Noticing is the first step back.

It's the middle of an ordinary day. Nothing is wrong, your inbox is under control, and your shoulders are still up near your ears. You sit down to rest and your body doesn't come with you. Somewhere you read that you need nervous system regulation, and you nodded, and you have no idea what to do with that.

Let's start there, because the phrase gets used far more often than it gets explained. If you're a beginner, you only need two things today: a rough map of the states your body moves through, and one small way of checking where you are.

What nervous system regulation means

Nervous system regulation is a loose, everyday phrase for how your body moves into a stress response and finds its way back out. It doesn't mean feeling calm all day, and it doesn't mean you stop getting activated. It means you stop staying there.

And if you've been stuck for a long time, please hear this: your nervous system isn't broken. It's been protecting you with the tools it had. The work now is giving it better ones.

Three states, as polyvagal theory maps them

The most familiar map comes from polyvagal theory, which Stephen Porges first set out in a 1994 presidential address, published in 1995. It describes three circuits in the autonomic nervous system, each tied to a different state (Porges, 2022). In everyday terms, they might feel like this:

Ventral vagal is safety and connection. Your breathing is easy, conversation feels natural, and problems feel solvable.

Sympathetic is fight or flight. Your heart works harder than the situation requires, your thoughts speed up, and your jaw, shoulders and belly brace without asking you first.

Dorsal vagal is shutdown. The world goes grey, flat and far away. You feel heavy and slow.

These are positions, not personality types.

In the theory, the sympathetic and dorsal responses are ways of surviving threat, so nobody is asking you to get rid of them. None of these states is your fault. All of them are information. The goal is more time in ventral, and an easier road back to it.

I want to be honest about the map, too. Polyvagal theory is a theory, and scientists still debate it. In a 2023 paper, the researcher Paul Grossman argued that each of its basic premises is "either untenable or highly implausible." Porges, for his part, has kept refining the theory and responding to its critics (Porges, 2025). So hold the three states as words for noticing your body, not as settled anatomy.

Why regulation starts in the body

Your vagus nerve is one of the main lines between your organs and your brain, and much of its traffic runs upward. It is the principal relay for sensory information from your lungs, and its sensory fibers are the main sensory supply to your heart (Prescott & Liberles, 2022).

So how you're breathing and how your heart is beating are part of what your brain is working with. If someone has ever told you to calm down and nothing happened, you already know that telling doesn't reach everything. Thinking work is valuable and it has its place. The body is simply another way in.

It's hard to change what you can't notice, which makes a check-in a kind place for a beginner to start.

The Nervous System Check-In, step by step

This is a practice from my book, a way of paying attention. It isn't a tested clinical protocol.

A safety note first: if you carry a trauma history or tend to feel floaty or disconnected when you turn your attention inward, go slowly. Keep your eyes open if closed eyes feel unsafe, and pause to look around the room any time it feels like too much.

Find a comfortable seat with your feet flat on the floor, or lie down if that's easier. The whole practice takes about 2 to 3 minutes. Give each of the first six steps about 10 seconds.

  1. Settle. Place both hands in your lap, palms up. Take one normal breath and notice it without changing it.
  2. Jaw. Is it clenched or easy? Notice whether your teeth are touching. No fixing yet.
  3. Shoulders. Are they lifted toward your ears?
  4. Belly. Is it soft or braced? Does your breath reach your lower abdomen, or does it stop at your chest?
  5. Heart area. Is there tightness, heaviness, openness or warmth? Put a word or a color to it, whatever arrives first.
  6. Name it. Which state are you closest to right now: ventral, sympathetic or dorsal? Just name it, with no judgment.
  7. Exhale. Take one slow breath out through slightly parted lips, longer than your inhale. Feel your shoulders drop even a millimeter. Return to the room.

What you might notice

You might be surprised by how much tension you're carrying. That isn't something to fix right now. You might struggle to locate any sensation at all, and that's allowed too. A small wave of emotion can surface as well.

If sitting upright is uncomfortable, try this on your back with your knees bent and your feet flat on the floor. If you feel overwhelmed or very floaty, open your eyes, look around the room and name five things you can see before you continue.

What regulation can feel like

It doesn't have to feel like bliss. It might be a small release of breath, your shoulders dropping half an inch, a moment of quiet between thoughts. Deb Dana calls these glimmers, "micro-moments of regulation that foster feelings of well-being," and part of this practice is learning to catch them before they pass unnoticed.

The first run doesn't make you a runner, but the first run still counts.

If you'd like a simple way to begin, try checking in three times today: morning, midday and evening. And this week, when a difficult email arrives and your chest tightens before you've finished reading, bring your attention to your exhale. One extended breath out, as slow as you can make it. Notice what your nervous system does.
