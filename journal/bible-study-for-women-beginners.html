Bible Study for Women: How to Begin When You're Hurting

A Bible study for women who are hurting: how one week of a six-week study works, and a twenty-minute first session in John 11.

By Hannah Grace · 8 min read

Begin with four verses and a notebook.

The Bible has been on the nightstand for weeks. You meant to open it. Then the hard thing happened, or kept happening, and now even the thought of a reading plan feels like one more place to fall behind.

If you are looking for a Bible study for women because something hurts and you don't know where to begin, begin smaller than you think. You need a Bible, a notebook and about twenty minutes. You do not need to know where Leviticus is, and you do not need to feel better first.

Below is how one week of a six-week study works, and a complete first session you can do today in John 11.

What a Bible study for women is, and what it isn't

A Bible study is slow reading with a pen in your hand. You read a passage, notice what it actually says, and answer it honestly. A devotional usually hands you a verse and a finished thought. In a study, you do part of the work.

For a woman who is hurting, that matters. A finished thought can feel like a bandage pressed on too fast. A passage read slowly, with room for your own question, meets you where you actually are. It will not fix the hard thing. It gives you a place to bring it.

How to start a Bible study: one week of read, reflect, respond

The session below comes from Healing Bible Study for Women, a six-week study in which each chapter is one week. The book doesn't use the words read, reflect and respond, but every week moves through those three, in roughly this order:

  • Read. The week opens with a memory verse. Then the chapter teaches through a few passages, often by staying with a woman in Scripture: Mary at her brother's tomb, the woman who reached for the hem, the woman at the well. A short sidebar called Digging Deeper is there for the woman who wants to go further.
  • Reflect. Five reflection questions, each meant to be answered in a few honest sentences. Some begin "In your journal," and some ask you to share with your group.
  • Respond. Written exercises with space for your answers, an Integration Bridge that gives you one small thing to try "this week" when a specific moment comes, and a closing prayer written in full.

The book gives one chapter to each week and sets no daily schedule, except in Week 3, where it asks for 15 to 20 minutes a day for a seven-day prayer. A Bible study for beginners does not need to be longer than that.

A first session for beginners: Mary at the tomb

Week 1 of the study is about the wound you stopped naming, and one of its passages is John 11. Lazarus has died, and his sister Mary comes to Jesus. It is a good first passage for a hurting heart because nobody in it is pretending. Mary did not tidy her grief before she brought it to Jesus.

Here are the four verses at the center of the scene:

"Then when Mary was come where Jesus was, and saw him, she fell down at his feet, saying unto him, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died. When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews also weeping which came with her, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled, And said, Where have ye laid him? They said unto him, Lord, come and see. Jesus wept." (John 11:32-35, KJV)

What you need: a Bible, a notebook and a pen. How long: about twenty minutes. If you only have ten, do steps 1 to 4 and stop there.

  1. Write the date and one line. At the top of the page, write "What I'm bringing today:" and finish it with a word. Grief. My marriage. I don't know. Any of those will do.
  2. Read the whole story once. Read John 11:17-35 at your normal pace, just to know what happens. Lazarus has been in the grave four days, and Martha meets Jesus on the road first.
  3. Read verses 32 to 35 again, slowly. Aloud if you can.
  4. Write down only what the text says. Four or five plain lines: where Mary goes, what she does, what she says, what Jesus asks, what He does. Feelings can wait for the next steps.
  5. Look back at verse 21. Martha said the same sentence on the road: "Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died." (John 11:21, KJV) Mary says it at His feet. With Martha, Jesus talks about the resurrection. With Mary, He asks one question and weeps. Underline the phrase in verses 32 to 35 that stopped you.
  6. Answer one question in your notebook. The book asks it right after this passage: "What wound have you been keeping at a distance from God because you thought you needed to process it more before you brought it to Him?" Three or four honest sentences are enough.
  7. Write your own sentence to Him. Begin with Mary's first word, "Lord," and say the true thing as plainly as she did. Jesus did not correct her for saying hers.
  8. Choose one small thing for this week. This is the study's Integration Bridge for Week 1: when someone asks how you are and you feel the pull to say "I'm good, just busy, trusting God," pause for two seconds and admit the truth to yourself, even silently.
  9. Close in prayer. Use your own words, or borrow a line from the book's closing prayer for this week: "You wept at a tomb knowing resurrection was coming. Weep with me now."

That is one sitting from the first week. Healing Bible Study for Women gives you six weeks of them: naming the wound you stopped naming, finding the lies that took root while you were hurting, the God who heals what medicine can't, forgiveness and what Scripture actually requires, rebuilding your identity, boundaries and spiritual rhythms with Nehemiah, and walking out whole with the woman who left her water jar. Each week has its memory verse, five reflection questions, written exercises and a closing prayer, and the paperback leaves lined space for your answers. A short conclusion helps you look back over all six weeks.

What you might notice

You may cry. Tears in a first session do not mean you did it wrong, and the passage itself is full of weeping. You may feel a little less alone with the hard thing.

You may also notice nothing at all. The words can look flat, and step 6 can stay unanswered. That is an honest result, and it needs no fixing. Write "nothing came today" and come back to the same four verses tomorrow.

A Bible study is no promise that the hurt will lift, and a hurt that stays says nothing about the size of your faith. If the pain is deep, or has gone on a long time, it can also deserve the help of a pastor, a counselor or a doctor. The book says it plainly: "Some wounds need more than a Bible study can give."

When to use it: alone, with a friend, or in a small group

  • Alone. Early, before the phone, or at night once the house is quiet. Leave the notebook where you will see it tomorrow.
  • With a friend. Read the passage aloud together, do steps 4 to 7 on your own, then share only what you want to. The book says naming is "more powerful when it's spoken aloud to someone who receives it well."
  • In a small group. The book's note for leaders: use the reflection questions as a discussion guide, one chapter a week, go slowly, keep what is shared in the room, and let women pass when a question is too much.

If step 7 brought up an old hurt that needs more than one sentence, how to lament with a written prayer from Lamentations 3 comes from the same first week. If you are waiting on a body to heal, these healing scriptures for the woman tired of asking come from Week 3. And on days a whole study feels like too much, these prayer journal ideas for when you don't know what to say give you a first line.

Questions women ask about Bible study for women

Where should a beginner start a Bible study? With one short passage and a notebook, not a whole book of the Bible. A scene like John 11:32-35 works well: read it twice, write down what it says, and answer one honest question.

How long should I study the Bible each day? About twenty minutes is enough for the session above, and ten will do if you stop after step 4. The study it comes from takes one chapter a week and, in Week 3, asks for 15 to 20 minutes a day.

Should I do a Bible study alone or in a group? Either works. Alone, you go at your own pace. With a friend or a small group, the reflection questions become a conversation, as long as everyone is free to pass.

The Bible on the nightstand is still there. Tonight it can lie open at four verses, with one honest line under them in your handwriting.
