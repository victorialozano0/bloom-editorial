It's 3 a.m. Again: Your Anxiety Isn't Imagined

Why perimenopause can set off your brain's alarm system out of nowhere, and why it was never a character flaw.

By Claire Harmon · 3 min read

It was never a character flaw.

It's 3 a.m. Your heart is hammering. You're lying in the dark, running through every possible reason: the 2 p.m. coffee, the odd email from your boss, something you said to your sister three weeks ago.

You are exhausted. Your mind will not stop.

By morning you've reached the usual verdict: something is wrong with me. Maybe you've even been told it's stress, and that you should try to worry less. (Helpful.)

Here's what I wish someone had told me sooner. You are not falling apart, and you are not suddenly fragile. What's happening has a biological explanation. You are not in your head. You are in perimenopause.

Your brain's calming system runs partly on estrogen

Think of GABA as your brain's volume knob for fear. When the amygdala, your brain's alarm center, starts firing, GABA steps in and turns the signal down. We're safe. Stand down.

Estrogen helps activate GABA receptors. And in perimenopause, estrogen doesn't decline in a tidy straight line. It fluctuates: high one week, low the next. Each dip leaves your alarm system with less buffering. That racing heart at 3 a.m. is your amygdala firing without enough GABA to quiet it.

Then there's progesterone, the part most women never hear about. Progesterone produces a compound called allopregnanolone, which binds to those same GABA receptors and produces calm. It is, in effect, your body's own built-in anti-anxiety compound. When progesterone drops, that calming signal drops with it.

You didn't suddenly get bad at life. You didn't lose your ability to cope. You lost the hormone that was helping you cope.

Why a Tuesday email now feels like an emergency

There's a third player: cortisol, your main stress hormone. Estrogen normally helps regulate the HPA axis, the control tower that decides when to release cortisol and, crucially, when to call it back. Perimenopause disrupts that calibration.

Researchers at the University of Pittsburgh (2021) tracked cortisol across menopausal stages and found that women in perimenopause had stronger cortisol responses to the same stressors, and took longer to return to baseline. Same life. Same stressor. A bigger, longer alarm.

Your body isn't overreacting. It's under-regulated. That difference matters, because it means the answer isn't trying harder to think calm thoughts. It's working with your nervous system, giving it signals it's no longer getting from steady hormones.

And if a doctor brushed this off as "just stress," that's worth a second conversation, ideally with someone who takes the hormonal side seriously. You are allowed to bring notes.

A small practice for tonight

Start building your own evidence. Grab a notebook and answer these, one per sitting if brain fog is loud today:

  • When did you first notice this anxiety, or notice it getting worse? A rough age or year is fine.
  • Does it follow any pattern in your cycle, or does it seem random?
  • What does it feel like in your body, before any thought shows up? Racing heart, tight chest, a drop in your stomach?

There's no right answer. You're drawing the first lines of a map, and that map makes the next doctor's visit a very different conversation.
