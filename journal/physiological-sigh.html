The Physiological Sigh: Calm in 90 Seconds

A two-breath tool for sudden perimenopause anxiety that works in a meeting, in the car, or at 3 a.m.

By Claire Harmon · 2 min read

The exhale is where the calm lives.

It's 2:47 on a Wednesday afternoon and you're in a meeting. Nothing dramatic is happening. And yet your chest tightens, your thoughts speed up, and you're suddenly sure you're about to cry, flee, or say something you'll regret.

This is perimenopause anxiety in its daytime outfit. And it often arrives when you can't exactly lie down on the conference room floor.

So here's the tool I reach for first. It takes about a minute, needs no equipment, and nobody around you will notice.

Why a breath can help

It's called the physiological sigh: two inhales stacked on top of each other, then one long, slow exhale. Researchers at Stanford (2023) compared it with mindfulness meditation and box breathing, and the sigh reduced self-reported anxiety faster than either.

The mechanism is refreshingly simple. The second little inhale re-inflates partially collapsed air sacs in your lungs, which makes room for a bigger exhale. And it's the exhale that activates your parasympathetic nervous system, the biological brake on your stress response.

The exhale is where the calm lives. In perimenopause, when cortisol spikes without estrogen there to buffer it, your body can stay in alarm mode longer than the moment deserves. The sigh interrupts that loop. You're not thinking your way out. You're breathing your way out.

The practice

Safety note: if you have asthma or any condition affecting your breathing, check with your doctor before changing your breathing pattern. You can return to normal breathing at any point.

Sit or stand comfortably. This takes one to two minutes.

  1. Take a full, normal inhale through your nose. (Count to 3.)
  2. Without exhaling, take one more quick sniff through your nose, stacking it on top of the first breath. (One second. Your lungs will feel full.)
  3. Open your mouth slightly. Exhale slowly and completely, letting all the air out. (Count to 6 or 8. Longer than feels natural.)
  4. Pause at the bottom of the exhale for one or two counts before your next breath.
  5. Repeat 2 to 4 times, then return to normal breathing.

What you might notice: a big involuntary sigh or yawn (good sign), a bit of lightheadedness after the first round (it passes within a breath), warmth in your chest or shoulders. Or nothing dramatic at all, which is also completely normal. Regulation is often quiet at first.

If the double inhale feels uncomfortable, try one deep inhale for 4 counts and a long exhale for 8. If you feel dizzy, open your eyes, look around the room, and name three things you can see before continuing.

Your 90 seconds this week

Pick one predictable moment, like the mid-afternoon slump when brain fog peaks and anxiety quietly rises. Before you reach for your phone or another coffee, do two physiological sighs. Then notice what your body does. No transformation required. Just 90 seconds of evidence.
